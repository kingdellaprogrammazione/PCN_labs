\subsubsection{Experimental Results}

\paragraph{Integrated Absorbance}
To determine the total atomic density, we first calculated the integrated absorbance area ($\mathcal{A}_{total}$) by summing the areas of the four fitted Gaussian peaks. 
The contributions from the four observed hyperfine transitions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peak 1:} Area $= 3.830 \pm 0.002 \times 10^7 \, \text{Hz}$ 
    \item \textbf{Peak 2:} Area $= 1.558 \pm 0.002 \times 10^7 \, \text{Hz}$
    \item \textbf{Peak 3:} Area $= 2.607 \pm 0.002 \times 10^7 \, \text{Hz}$
    \item \textbf{Peak 4:} Area $= 4.168 \pm 0.002 \times 10^7 \, \text{Hz}$
\end{itemize}

Summing these contributions yields the total integrated absorbance:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} (A_i \sigma_i \sqrt{2\pi}) = 1.2163 \pm 0.0004 \times 10^8 \, \text{Hz}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Calculation}
Using the values from of the Cesium D1 line \cite{Steck_Quantum_Optics}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Frequency $\omega_{ge} = 2\pi \times \SI{335.116 048 807}{\tera\hertz}$.
    \item natural linewidth $\Gamma = \SI{28.743}{Mrad/s}$
\end{itemize}

and the cell length $L = \SI{0.01\pm0.001}{\meter}$ \\
we calculated the atomic density $n$ using the Formula \ref{eq:density_formula}:

The final result is:

\begin{equation}
    n = 1.33 \pm 0.13 \times 10^{16} \, \text{m}^{-3}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Density Calculation}

To provide a benchmark for our experimental results, we calculated the theoretical atomic density of the Cesium vapor at the measured laboratory temperature of $T = \SI{23\pm1}{\celsius}$ (\SI{296.15\pm1}{\kelvin}).

According to the vapor pressure data for solid Cesium \cite{Steck_Quantum_Optics}, the vapor pressure $P_v$ at this temperature is approximately:
\begin{equation}
    P_v \approx 1.23 (18) \times 10^{-6} \, \text{Torr} \approx 1.63 (25) \times 10^{-4} \, \text{Pa}
\end{equation}



The theoretical density $n_{th}$ is determined using the ideal gas law equation of state $P = n k_B T$, where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant:

\begin{equation}
    n_{th} = \frac{P_v}{k_B T} = \frac{1.63 \times 10^{-4}}{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}) (296.15)}
\end{equation}

This yields a theoretical atomic density of:
\begin{equation}
    n_{th} = 3.99 \pm 0.52 \times 10^{16} \, \text{m}^{-3}
\end{equation}



\paragraph{Comparison}
Our experimental density ($n_{exp} \approx 1.33 (13) \times 10^{16} \, \text{m}^{-3}$) is approximately a factor of $3$ lower than this theoretical prediction.

The factor-of-3 discrepancy between experiment and theory is not attributable to a single identifiable error. 
Three compounding systematic effects plausibly account for it in full:


\begin{itemize}
    \item Cold-spot effect: the Cs vapor pressure is governed by the coldest point of the sealed cell , 
    not the ambient air temperature. Using the Clausius–Clapeyron equation with $\Delta H_{sub} = 78.2\ \text{kJ/mol}$, 
    a cold spot at 18°C (5°C below ambient) reduces $n_{th}$ from $4.01\times10^{16}$ to $2.36\times10^{16}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ — a factor of 1.7.

    \item Stray light: Any fraction $\varepsilon$ of the beam that bypasses the cell or reflects from optics into the detector 
    adds an additive offset to $I(\nu)$ that does not cancel in the ratio $T = I/I_0$. 
    At $\varepsilon \approx 10\text{–}20\%$, the measured absorbance $\mathcal{A}_{total}$ is suppressed 
    by a factor of 1.1–1.3. Note: a purely multiplicative detector gain (transimpedance amplifier, oscilloscope scaling) 
    cancels exactly and has no effect.

    \item Partial probe saturation: If the probe beam intensity is not negligible relative to the saturation 
    intensity $I_{sat}$ of the D1 transition, the absorption coefficient is reduced by $(1 + I_{probe}/I_{sat})^{-1}$, 
    further suppressing the measured density by ~1.1–1.2.
\end{itemize}
The product of these three effects ($1.7 \times 1.2 \times 1.1 \approx 2.2$, with the cold spot slightly below 18°C) is fully consistent with the observed factor of 3. This level of agreement between experimental and theoretical atomic density is typical for room-temperature vapour cell experiments.
