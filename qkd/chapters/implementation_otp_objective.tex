\subsection{One Time Pad}

The One-Time Pad (OTP) is a symmetric encryption scheme that provides
perfect secrecy, provided that the encryption key is truly random, at
least as long as the plaintext, kept secret, and used only once. Let
$P \in \{0,1\}^n$ denote the plaintext and $K \in \{0,1\}^n$ the random key.
Encryption is performed by computing the bitwise exclusive OR (XOR):

\[
  C = P \oplus K,
\]

where $C$ is the resulting ciphertext. Decryption uses the same key and the
same operation:

\[
  P = C \oplus K.
\]

Since XOR is its own inverse, applying the key a second time recovers the
original plaintext. Under the OTP assumptions, every possible plaintext is
equally likely for a given ciphertext, making the scheme
information-theoretically
secure. However, the practical use of OTP is limited by the difficulty of
securely generating, distributing, and storing one-time random keys of the same
length as the messages they protect.
