This part of the experience has the aim of showing the conceptual and
physical realization
of a system able to execute a schematic version of the BB84 protocol
TODO reference,
with some caveats.
Briefly, BB84 allows two parties to agree on a random binary string
(that can then be used as a key), detecting eavesdropping attemps
with reasonably high probability thanks to quantum mechanical laws.
It is important to notice that BB84 does not allow to generate a
secret key in any instance; for example, assuming a permanent
eavesdropper, a key will be generated, but the presence of the
attacker will be detected and the key thus discarded.

What is shown in this particular work
is a simple BB84 demonstration without the use of single photons (not
  produced nor
measured) due to the complexity and cost of the
required setup.
Much bigger light signals are used; nevertheless, the key
establishment setup is exactly the same as a simple free-space qkd
scheme, with polarizing beam splitters and half-wave plates. The
difference  lies in the fact that the detectors employed are designed
to detect macroscopic signals
and are calibrated to simulate the behaviour of single photon
detectors, meaning that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item if the light reaching the two receiving detectors is of
    approximately equal intensity an electronic system will make them
    emit a random "click", to simulate the random collapse of a
    single photon's wavefunction on one detector when it is in a
    superposition of paths;
  \item if the intensity reaching the two receiving detectors is
    clearly skewed between them the electronic system interpret this
    as if the photon's wavefunction is completely concentrated into
    one of the two final paths. The corresponding detector will
    "click" with certainty.
\end{itemize}
Notice that since the BB84 protocol's security relies on the quantum
nature of the physical particles involved, this demonstration miss
this property. For example, a photon splitting attack (PNS) (TODO:
put the ref) could be carried out easily, tapping the free-space line
and extracting a group of photons that make possible to know the
final synthethised key by a third party.

This experience allows to understand so both the theoretical
framework and the physical setup needed, giving to the experimenters
the possibility to get familiar with the typical issues that can
arise on an optical table.
