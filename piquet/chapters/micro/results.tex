Following development, the patterned wafer was inspected under a bright-field
optical microscope and the critical dimensions of the developed features were
measured using the microscope's built-in calibrated distance tool.

\subsubsection*{Optical microscope inspection}

In bright-field illumination, the image-reversal photoresist layer appears bright
(yellow--pink under the cleanroom sodium-vapour ambient illumination) owing to
thin-film interference within the $\sim$1.3\,$\mu$m resist film, while the
exposed silicon substrate in the developed windows appears dark; this colour contrast
allows direct identification of the remaining resist regions and of the open windows
where the resist has been fully removed by the developer. The wafer showed a
well-resolved pattern consisting of regular arrays of square and circular windows,
parallel line trenches at nominal feature sizes of 2\,$\mu$m, 3\,$\mu$m, and
5\,$\mu$m, together with alignment marks and contact-pad structures, all consistent
with the expected mask layout \cite{FundTechProc2024}; representative micrographs of the 2\,$\mu$m and 3\,$\mu$m window arrays are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:2um_mask} and \ref{fig:3um_mask}.

\subsubsection*{Critical-dimension analysis}

To quantify the fidelity of the pattern transfer we measured the width of the
developed windows at three nominal feature sizes using calibrated distance markers
placed across feature edges in the optical microscope software. The results are
summarised in Table~\ref{tab:cd_bias}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{rrrr}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Nominal width ($\mu$m)} & \textbf{Measured width ($\mu$m)}
      & \textbf{CD bias ($\mu$m)} \\
    \midrule
    2 & 2.14 & $+0.14$ \\
    3 & 3.10 & $+0.10$ \\
    5 & 5.30 & $+0.30$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Nominal mask dimension, measured window width, and critical-dimension
           (CD) bias for the three feature sizes inspected under the optical
           microscope. CD bias is defined as measured width $-$ nominal width.}
  \label{tab:cd_bias}
\end{table}

In all three cases the measured windows are wider than the corresponding mask
apertures, yielding a consistently positive CD bias in the range
$+0.10$\,$\mu$m to $+0.30$\,$\mu$m. In contact printing, the mask chrome edge is
not a perfectly sharp optical boundary: diffraction of the UV illumination
(I-line, 365\,nm) around the chrome features creates a fringe of partial exposure
extending slightly beyond the nominal aperture edge. In \emph{image-reversal} mode
this fringe effect acts to narrow the cross-linked (insoluble) zone, leaving a
correspondingly wider region sensitised by the flood exposure and subsequently
dissolved by the developer. The ${\approx}1.4\,\mu$m minimum resolvable period $2b_\mathrm{min}$ characterises the global resolution limit of the contact-printing geometry, not the magnitude of the CD bias itself; the positive bias is more directly related to the width of the diffraction-broadened edge transition at the mask aperture, which corresponds to a fraction of $2b_\mathrm{min}$ and is therefore qualitatively consistent with sub-micron edge broadening, even though the full resolution limit exceeds the $+0.10$--$+0.30$\,$\mu$m biases observed by more than an order of magnitude \cite{FundTechProc2024}. A secondary contribution may
arise from slight over-development: extending the immersion time in the AZ\,400K
developer beyond the dose-to-clear point attacks the cross-linked resist sidewalls
laterally, broadening the windows at the expense of sidewall verticality.

\subsubsection*{Profilometer step-height measurement}

While the optical microscope quantifies the \emph{lateral} fidelity of the pattern
transfer, it cannot resolve the \emph{vertical} profile of the developed windows. A
contact-stylus profilometer was therefore used to scan across individual trench
edges and recover the height difference between the remaining resist ("land") and
the exposed silicon floor, providing a direct check on the $\sim$1.3\,$\mu$m nominal
resist thickness established in Table~3.2. We exported the \texttt{.dat} binary
files of the profilometer results however it was a KLA P-17 specific binary format. The trace was therefore reconstructed offline using
the third-party open-source parser of Du~\cite{P7Dat2Txt}, which decodes the raw
fixed-point $Z$-samples but does not carry over the profilometer's scan-length or
scan-speed setting; converting the \texttt{.dat} file to a plottable trace this way
recovers the $Z$-height values themselves but loses the calibration needed to
convert the sample index into a physical distance, which is why the horizontal
axis of the resulting traces is reported in raw sample points rather than in
micrometres. The resulting $Z$-height trace was referenced (zeroed) to the first
sampled point of the scan, so that the vertical axis reports height \emph{relative
to the scan's starting land},
not an absolute wafer elevation; trench floors therefore appear as negative
excursions and raised regions as positive ones. Before extracting any height value,
the decoded trace was cross-checked against the same data re-read from its saved
text export: the residual between the two, shown in the lower panel of
Figures~\ref{fig:profilometer_wide} and~\ref{fig:profilometer_narrow},
remains below $5\times10^{-4}$\,nm throughout, several orders of magnitude smaller
than the step height itself. This confirms that the discrepancy is pure
floating-point rounding introduced by the text export and not an experimental
artefact, validating the decoding pipeline ahead of any physical interpretation of
the trace.

In both scans the trace does not return to its starting level after crossing the
trench: the land on the far side of the step is measured 100--200\,nm higher than
the land where the scan began. Because the $Z$-axis is zeroed to the first sample
rather than to an independent reference, this offset is consistent with a slight
tilt of the wafer relative to the stylus scan direction (an uneven mount on the
profilometer stage) rather than with a genuine difference in resist thickness
between the two lands; the trench depth is therefore read as the drop from the
\emph{near} land to the floor, not from the two lands' average.

The measured trench depths, $\approx$730\,nm (wide trench) and
$\approx$660\,nm (narrow trench), are both shallower than the nominal
1.3\,$\mu$m resist thickness, recovering only 50--55\,\% of the nominal film. This
is consistent with the same edge-broadening mechanisms invoked in the
critical-dimension analysis above: the diffraction-broadened, sloped sidewall at
the mask aperture edge means the stylus, which has a finite tip radius, cannot
follow a perfectly vertical wall down to the true trench floor and instead
integrates over the sloped transition, systematically under-reporting the true
step height. A shallower-than-nominal reading is also consistent with the
over-development mechanism proposed earlier: incomplete clearing of the
cross-linked sidewall near the floor would leave a thin residual resist layer
that reduces the apparent trench depth without being visible under the optical
microscope. Even if the parser had preserved the scan-length setting, the
2.14\,$\mu$m and 3.10\,$\mu$m window pitches measured optically in
Figures~\ref{fig:2um_mask} and~\ref{fig:3um_mask} could not have been used to
calibrate the horizontal axis, since both profilometer traces cross a single
isolated trench edge rather than a periodic window array.

\subsubsection*{Process failures and defect analysis}

Not all wafers processed during the session yielded acceptable results; two failure
modes were observed and are documented below.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{case_of_bad_lith_1.jpeg}
    \caption{Wafer exhibiting incomplete pattern transfer: the patterned array of
             square windows is visible only in the lower portion of the wafer, while
             the upper region shows a uniformly intact resist film with no developed
             features.}
    \label{fig:bad_lith_1}
  \end{minipage}
  \hfill
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{case_of_bad_lith_2.jpeg}
    \caption{Wafer exhibiting spin-coating defects: radial comet streaks originate
             from contaminating particles and extend across the resist film, together
             with visible dust deposits that disrupt the local resist uniformity.}
    \label{fig:bad_lith_2}
  \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:bad_lith_1} shows a wafer on which development occurred only in the
lower portion: the square-window array is well resolved there, while the upper half
of the wafer retains an intact, undeveloped resist film. The sharp horizontal
boundary between the developed and undeveloped zones is inconsistent with a gradual
developer-concentration gradient and instead points to an incomplete exposure: if the
mercury-lamp illumination or the mask aperture did not cover the entire wafer during
the exposure step, only the illuminated portion would undergo the photochemical
conversion required for image-reversal; the unexposed upper region would remain
insoluble after PEB and flood exposure alike, preventing development. Ensuring full
and uniform illumination across the wafer surface, and confirming mask-to-wafer
contact over the entire area before triggering the exposure, is therefore critical
to avoid this failure mode.

Figure~\ref{fig:bad_lith_2} shows a second failure mode attributable to
contamination during spin coating: radial streaks (\emph{comet} defects) extend
across the resist film from the wafer centre, together with isolated dust deposits
visible as bright spots \cite{FundTechProc2024}. Comet defects arise when a
contaminating particle is present on the wafer surface at the moment the liquid
resist is dispensed; the centrifugal flow of the resist around the obstacle creates
a local disruption in the otherwise radially symmetric film, producing a streak of
non-uniform thickness that persists through the soft bake and exposure steps. The
consequences for the final pattern are twofold: within the streak, the resist
thickness deviates from the nominal 1.3\,$\mu$m and the local exposure dose is no
longer optimised, leading to under- or over-development; beneath a dust particle the
resist may be lifted during development, leaving a pinhole. Both effects render
the patterned area in the defect zone unsuitable for subsequent processing. To
prevent this failure mode, wafers and all handling surfaces must be thoroughly
cleaned and inspected under cleanroom lighting before spin coating, and the spin
coater bowl should be purged of any debris between runs.

The two failure modes documented above are not exhaustive; photolithography is susceptible to a wider range of defect types that were not observed in this group's micrographs but are worth noting for context. \textit{Photomask-inherent defects} (such as pinholes in the chrome absorber, extra-chrome spots, or scratches on the mask surface introduced during handling or storage) print directly into the resist pattern and propagate to every wafer processed with that mask without correction. \textit{Localised solvent damage} (e.g.\ a droplet of PGMEA or developer contacting the spun film before the soft bake) can dissolve the resist in a concentrated spot, creating a clearing surrounded by a ring of re-deposited thickened material. \textit{Thin-film interference artefacts} arise when the resist deviates locally from its nominal thickness: the reflectance colour viewed under bright-field illumination shifts, and the local exposure dose is altered because the effective optical path through the resist changes, leading to regions of systematic under- or over-development within an otherwise correctly exposed wafer. These failure modes are well documented in the photolithography literature \cite{FundTechProc2024} and are included here as contextual completeness; they were not present in the micrographs collected by this group during this session.
