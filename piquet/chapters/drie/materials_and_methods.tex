% OBJECTIVE
\subsubsection{Objective}
In this section a discussion of the final treatment the wafers' micro
and nanostructures
developed during the previous experiences will be held. In particular,
a Pseudo-Bosch process will be acted on the structures, allowing a
cost-effective way to
obtain high aspect ratios geometries preserving also precision.
After that, a complete inspection with the single electron microscope
(SEM) will be carried out
to ensure the effectiveness of the treatment.

% SETUP DESCRIPTION AND LIST OF COMPONENTS
\subsubsection{Setup description and list of components}

Below, a complete list of machines and instruments used to carry out
the activities is presented.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Diamond-tipped scribe (used to score and cleave the silicon wafers
    into chip fragments along the (100) crystal planes);
  \item 2\,\si{\micro\meter} \ce{SiO2} hard-mask layer on the micro-sample
    wafer, providing improved etch selectivity;
  \item Thermoplastic mounting wax (hardens at \SI{70}{\degreeCelsius}),
    used to bond chip fragments to the carrier wafer;
  \item \textbf{ICP-DRIE system}: PlasmaPro~100 Cobra (Oxford Instruments) \cite{PlasmaProCobra}; inductively coupled plasma reactor used for the Pseudo-Bosch etch of both micro and nano samples;
  \item \textbf{Dual-beam SEM/FIB}: FEI Quanta~3D FEG \cite{QuantaFEG} (Thermo Fisher Scientific); integrates a field-emission SEM column with a gallium-ion FIB column, a positively-biased Everhart--Thornley secondary-electron detector (ETD), and a Peltier-cooled stage. Used at 20\,kV for post-etch cross-section imaging in this experience (electron column only; the FIB column was not operated during the SEM imaging session).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{SEM imaging principle}

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) used for post-etch characterisation is a \textit{field-emission gun} (FEG) instrument. In a FEG-SEM, electrons are extracted from a sharp tungsten tip by a high electric field; the resulting beam has a very small energy spread and can be focused to a spot smaller than 5\,nm, giving the high spatial resolution required to resolve sub-100\,nm features. The focused beam is raster-scanned across the sample surface by electromagnetic deflection coils, and at each pixel the number of \textit{secondary electrons} (SE, low-energy electrons knocked out of the sample surface by the primary beam) is detected by an Everhart--Thornley or equivalent detector. Because secondary-electron yield depends strongly on the local surface inclination and material composition, the collected SE signal maps the surface topography with high contrast: steep sidewalls appear bright, flat horizontal surfaces appear darker. This secondary-electron contrast mechanism is what gives SEM images their characteristic three-dimensional, shadowed appearance and makes it possible to measure trench depth, sidewall angle, and surface roughness directly from the micrographs. For the cross-section imaging used in this experience, the sample was first cleaved perpendicular to the trench direction (as described in the process flow below) to expose a fresh, uncontaminated sidewall surface; the chip was then mounted at a 52$^\circ$ stage tilt to bring the cleaved cross-section into the field of view of the column.

% PROCESS FLOW DESCRIPTION
\subsubsection{Process flow description}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wafer scribing and cleaving}: Both wafers were scored
    with a diamond-tipped scribe along a chosen crystal direction and then
    cleaved by applying controlled pressure, breaking along the (100) crystal
    plane. This produces flat, straight chip edges suitable for SEM
    cross-section imaging of the sidewall profiles.

  \item \textbf{Wafer mounting}: The cleaved chip fragments were bonded
    onto a 4-inch carrier wafer using the thermoplastic wax (applied liquid at
    \SI{70}{\degreeCelsius} and allowed to harden on cooling), enabling the
    small fragments to be processed in the ICP-DRIE reactor whose chuck is
    designed for full 4-inch wafers.

  \item \textbf{DRIE machine cleaning}: The main chamber of the DRIE
    machine must be cleaned at the beginning of each day to ensure minimal
    contamination from residual gases and organic deposits. To that end, an
    oxygen (\ce{O2}) plasma cleaning recipe is run before any sample is
    processed: the oxygen plasma removes organic residues (resist fragments,
    hydrocarbons) from the chamber walls and the sample chuck, ensuring a
    clean, reproducible starting condition for subsequent etch runs.

  \item \textbf{Pseudo Bosch process}: Then the Pseudo Bosch deep reactive
    ion etching (DRIE)
    process can start.
    This is a common method of dry etching, that encompasses all the
    etching-ways that involve ion bombardment, usually from a plasma of
    reactive-element gaseous compositions.
    Before diving into the Pseudo Bosch process, let's analyze in
    general DRIE process and
    its wider belonging class made by the reactive ion
    etching (RIE) processes.

    RIE process' aim is the precision etching of structures without undercuts, which isotropic wet etching cannot achieve because it removes material at a similar rate in all directions. Anisotropic (crystallographic) wet etches such as KOH or TMAH on specific silicon planes can produce directional sidewalls, but they are constrained to the fixed geometric angles imposed by the crystal lattice and cannot produce the arbitrary range of geometries that RIE supports.
    Usually RIE is used to excavate shallow features mantaining a
    good verticality along the walls and at the same time a optimal
    depth control at
    a fast etching speed (TODO:picture).
    The first step involves the creation of a plasma. Let's recall
    that a plasma is a state of matter
    originated from a locally electrically neutral gas that has been
    ionized (partly or entirely). This is made possible by the employment of
    a \SI{13.56}{\mega \hertz} radio
    frequency (RF) electromagnetic field, that
    with its continuous stimulus manages to ionize, effectively using
    the cascade effect, a neutral gas. Notice that the gas and the
    wafer are collocated in this little cavity between the two RF electrodes.
    The free electrons can than be captured either by an electrode,
    or by the wafer standing over the other one. The first ones are fed to
    ground, while the second ones causes the wafer to become charged negatively.
    The ionized gas, in contrast, loses electrons and become slightly
    positive-charged. Indeed, notice that the RF field can accelerate
    in a meaningful
    way only the electrons, not the gas ions, because of their
    bigger mass. This configurations results in a net acceleration experimented
    by the ions towards the fixed wafer, that experiences the "bombardment"
    cited before. The bombardment can affect the exposed wafer
    surface in two ways,
    both chemically and mechanically. Indeed the wafer can experience both
    chemical processes with the highly reactive gas molecules and sputtering
    (that is the detachment of molecules associated by a transfer of
    kinetic energy)
    due to the speed of the ions.
    From this description the anisotropic characteristics of RIE
    can be understood, since the ions have almost normal trajectories
    with respect
    to the wafer plane. In this regard, notice that plasma density affects the
    degree of anisotropy. The chemical species present in the plasma can be
    monitored in real time by \textit{optical emission spectroscopy} (OES): every
    excited species emits a characteristic optical spectrum, allowing the operator
    to identify reaction products and track the etch endpoint, defined as the moment
    at which one material layer is fully consumed and the underlying material is exposed.

    The distinction between RIE and DRIE lies in the etch depth achieved: DRIE
    is used where depths beyond what RIE can practically sustain are required,
    producing structures with aspect ratios up to 1:100, such as deep trenches
    and vertical pillars.

    \paragraph*{Etch chemistry.}
    Two gases drive the process chemistry. \textit{Sulphur hexafluoride}
    (\ce{SF6}) is the primary silicon etch gas: in the plasma it dissociates
    into fluorine radicals (\ce{F^{.}}) and sulphur ions (\ce{S+}).
    Fluorine radicals react spontaneously and isotropically with silicon,
    forming volatile silicon tetrafluoride (\ce{SiF4}) that is pumped out of the
    chamber:
    \begin{equation*}
      \ce{Si + 4F^{.} -> SiF4 ^}
    \end{equation*}
    This reaction alone would etch isotropically in all directions; the
    directional substrate bias provides the anisotropic component. Not all
    materials can be etched with fluorine chemistry: aluminium fluoride
    (\ce{AlF3}) is non-volatile and would accumulate on the surface rather than
    being pumped away, making \ce{SF6} unsuitable for aluminium; similarly,
    GaAs requires chlorine-based chemistry for the same reason.

    \textit{Octafluorocyclobutane} (\ce{C4F8}) is the passivation gas: in the
    plasma it breaks into \ce{CF2} radicals that polymerise on all exposed
    surfaces, depositing a thin fluorocarbon film. On the sidewalls of a trench
    this film remains intact because there is no ion bombardment directed at
    vertical surfaces; on the trench bottom the ion bombardment physically
    sputters the passivation film away, re-exposing the silicon to fluorine
    attack. This selective removal of the bottom passivation layer is the
    mechanism that gives the Bosch and Pseudo-Bosch processes their anisotropy.

    The Bosch process is a common DRIE process that involves a repetition of two
    essential steps:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item an etching process, analogue to the one cited for RIE;
      \item a passivation step, where the exposed surfaces are
        covered with a thin strate
        of protecting material.
    \end{itemize}
    The passivation step might appear unnecessary given that the RIE ion
    bombardment is already largely directional; however, two mechanisms cause
    sidewall attack even in directional plasmas:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Isotropic chemical etching:} neutral reactive radicals generated
        in the plasma are not directed by the RF field and therefore attack all
        exposed surfaces, including the sidewalls;
      \item \textbf{Ion scattering:} sputtered material can be redirected at
        oblique angles, causing lateral erosion of the feature walls.
    \end{itemize}
    An example Bosch process recipe is given in \cref{tab:bosch_process}. The RF
    field is maintained throughout all three steps to sustain the plasma
    continuously.
    The first step is to introduce \ce{C4F8}, octafluorocyclobutane, in the
    chamber. This is made into a plasma and during this step a bond
    breaks, making the molecule linear.
    This polymer deposits for \SI{3}{\second} on every exposed
    surface of the wafer, protecting it.
    Then, the chamber is evacuated and \ce{SF6}, sulfur hexafluoride,
    is introduced.
    Plasma is created and an additional DC bias is applied, allowing
    an highly effective
    anisotropic etching of the passivated bottom of the wafer.
    The last step comes with DC turned off and a less directional
    plasma etch, caused
    by highly reactive flourine radicals that interact in a more
    isotropic way with the silicon.
    causing curved undulations (scallops) on the sidewalls.

    A problem with the Bosch process, especially if the fabrication consists in
    nanometer-level geometries, are the scallops, periodic undulations of
    several hundred nanometres amplitude that form along the sidewalls.
    For example, following the recipe of \cref{tab:bosch_process}, a scallop
    extends for hundreds of \si{\nano \meter}. To avoid this problem,
    a hybrid approach is used; this is called Pseudo Bosch process.
    It consists in sending both the \ce{SF6} and \ce{C4F8} at the
    same time into the chamber, adding \ce{CHF3} (trifluoromethane) and
    tuning down the table RF power to \SI{8}{\watt}. Because all three gases
    are present simultaneously, passivation deposition and silicon etching occur
    concurrently and reach a dynamic equilibrium at the surface: there is never
    a pure deposition phase or a pure etch phase, so the sidewall profile is
    smooth and scallops are eliminated. The \ce{CHF3} provides an additional
    fluorocarbon passivation source that coats the walls more uniformly,
    further improving sidewall smoothness. The directional etch component is
    the slow, ion-driven bombardment at \SI{8}{\watt} RF, which removes the
    bottom passivation film while leaving the sidewalls protected.

    The trade-off for this precision is throughput: under otherwise equivalent
    conditions the Pseudo-Bosch process reaches a depth of $\sim$\SI{1}{\micro
    \meter} in the time the standard Bosch process would etch $\sim$\SI{6}{\micro
    \meter}. This reduced rate is acceptable for nano-scale structures where
    surface quality matters more than throughput.

    The wafer is kept cold throughout the process by helium (He) backside
    cooling gas: He is introduced between the chuck and the wafer backside,
    conducting heat away and holding the wafer at a controlled low temperature.
    Reducing the wafer temperature suppresses the isotropic chemical component
    of the etch (fluorine-radical attack, which is thermally activated) and
    enhances sidewall passivation. At sufficiently low temperatures
    (around $-130\,^\circ$C, the cryogenic DRIE variant), a protective
    condensation film forms naturally on the sidewalls even without \ce{C4F8}
    injection; in our Pseudo-Bosch recipe the chiller is held at 5\,$^\circ$C,
    providing a milder version of this effect.

    The etch selectivity with respect to the mask material quantifies
    how much silicon is removed in the depth direction per unit of mask
    consumed. In the case of the
    photoresist mask (MICRO sample), the selectivity is approximately 1:10
    (10\,\si{\micro\meter} of silicon removed per 1\,\si{\micro\meter} of
    resist consumed). The 50\,nm copper hard mask used for the NANO sample
    achieves a far higher selectivity of $\sim$1:100, which is essential for
    deep nano-scale features where a photoresist mask would be consumed before
    the target depth is reached. Metals suitable as hard masks must not form
    volatile fluorides; copper satisfies this condition, whereas many other
    metals do not.

    A critical size-dependent effect that distinguishes nano-scale etching from
    micro-scale etching is the \textit{Aspect Ratio Dependent Etching} (ARDE)
    effect: narrower, deeper features experience reduced gas-phase transport and
    lower ion flux at the feature bottom compared with wider features etched
    under identical conditions. As a result, the local etch rate decreases as
    the aspect ratio increases, meaning that the micro-scale trenches and the
    nano-scale rings etch at different rates and develop different sidewall
    profiles even within the same process run.

  \item \textbf{Masking considerations}: The etch mask that protects the
    silicon during etching must itself withstand the etch chemistry. The two
    samples use different mask materials reflecting their different scale
    requirements. For the MICRO sample, an AZ\,5214E photoresist mask
    (organic polymer) was used directly, with a 2\,\si{\micro\meter} \ce{SiO2}
    hard-mask layer underneath to improve selectivity, adequate for shallow
    micro-scale etches. For the NANO sample, a 50\,nm copper (Cu) hard mask
    deposited and patterned by EBL lift-off was used, providing the durability
    required for deep, high-aspect-ratio nano-scale etches (selectivity values
    are given above). Small chip fragments from both samples were mounted on a
    4-inch carrier wafer using a wax that hardens at \SI{70}{\degreeCelsius},
    enabling the fragments to be handled in a reactor designed for full wafers.

  \item \textbf{Wax removal}: After etching, the chip fragments were
    detached from the carrier wafer by dissolving the mounting wax with
    water. The water was applied as a small droplet at the lateral edge of
    each chip, keeping the liquid below the patterned surface to avoid
    contaminating the etched structures; the chips were then gently slid
    from the carrier to complete the detachment.

  \item \textbf{Cross-section cleave}: After etching, the chips were
    re-scribed and cleaved a second time perpendicular to the trench direction
    in order to expose a fresh cross-section of the etched sidewalls for SEM
    imaging of the sidewall profile and etch depth.

\end{itemize}
