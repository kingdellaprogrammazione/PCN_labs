\textbf{Starting Material and Resist Setup} \\
The fabrication process was performed on a silicon substrate. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)
was used as the lithographic resist. PMMA is a positive-tone, high-resolution resist suited to
electron beam exposure: under electron bombardment, the high-energy electrons break the long
polymer chains into shorter, soluble fragments, allowing selective dissolution of the irradiated
regions during development. The resist was spun onto the silicon to a thickness of 300\,nm.

\textbf{Design and Proximity Effect Correction} \\
The target structures were split-ring resonators designed to function as nanoscale optical antennas
with feature gaps of 100\,nm. The pattern layout was generated as a CAD file (.gds format) and
included auxiliary structures to assess the achievable resolution.
To account for electron scattering and diffusion within the resist (the proximity effect), the design was 
imported into \textit{Beamer} software. The software performs Monte Carlo simulations of the electron trajectories in the specific resist/substrate stack (300\,nm PMMA, 50\,keV, 50\,kV emission), computing the spatial dose distribution for every shape in the layout and then adjusting the dose assigned to each sub-region so that the total received dose is uniform across all features. Corners and isolated features receive a higher dose correction factor ($\times$1.5), while dense central regions receive a lower factor ($\times$0.7), compensating for the additional backscatter contribution from neighbouring structures. The corrected layout was exported in \texttt{.CAN} format for the EBL machine.

\textbf{EBL Machine Preparation} \\
Prior to writing, the sample was mounted and the beam was calibrated to ensure accurate pattern transfer. The PMMA-coated silicon chip was fixed upside-down on a silicon carrier wafer using conducting adhesive tape; electrical grounding is essential because charge accumulation on an insulating surface during exposure deflects the incoming electron beam, distorting the written pattern. The carrier wafer was loaded into the EBL load-lock chamber, vented to atmosphere for loading; once sealed, the load-lock was evacuated and the sample was transferred into the main column chamber maintained at high vacuum ($\sim$10$^{-6}$\,mbar).

The beam current was measured using a \textit{Faraday cup}, a metallic cavity that captures every incident electron and converts the accumulated charge to a measurable current; the measured current in this session was $1.08 \pm 0.01$\,nA at 50\,kV accelerating voltage. This value is essential for calculating the exact dose delivered to the resist (dose = current $\times$ dwell time / pixel area). An auto-calibration routine was then run to correct for beam drift and to confirm the correct aperture pinhole.

Focus and astigmatism were corrected using a reference sample carrying gold nanospheres ($\sim$10\,nm diameter) deposited on silicon. The nanospheres act as high-contrast point scatterers that reveal the true beam shape: the objective lens current was adjusted until the nanosphere images were minimised to a point (focus), and the stigmator coils were tuned until the beam cross-section was perfectly circular (astigmatism correction). An astigmatic beam produces elliptical rather than round features, which would break the rotational symmetry of the split-ring gaps and shift the plasmonic resonance frequency. Finally, a \textit{plunge} correction compensated for height variations across the sample surface, and a \textit{stitching} correction aligned adjacent write fields by writing test marks at field boundaries on a calibration chip and measuring their positional error.

\textbf{EBL Exposure and Dose Calibration} \\
Patterning was performed using the EBL system at an accelerating voltage of 50\,kV. To determine
the optimal dose for this PMMA batch and development condition, a dose array was exposed: nine
identical split-ring resonator arrays were written sequentially, with the electron dose stepped
from 400\,$\mu$C/cm$^2$ to 800\,$\mu$C/cm$^2$ in increments of 50\,$\mu$C/cm$^2$. The total
exposure time was approximately 35\,min at a writing speed of 23\,mm/s.

\textbf{Development} \\
Following exposure, the sample was developed to dissolve the chain-scission fragments. The substrate
was immersed in a 1:3 (v/v) solution of MIBK (methyl isobutyl ketone) and isopropanol for exactly
50\,s; the sample was then immediately rinsed in pure isopropanol to halt development and dried
under a gentle stream of nitrogen (\ce{N2}).

\textbf{Metallization and Lift-off} \\
Once the PMMA pattern was developed, 50\,nm (0.5\,k\AA) of gold was deposited over the entire
sample surface by thermal evaporation; the deposited thickness was monitored in real time using a
quartz crystal microbalance. Lift-off was then performed by immersing the sample in an acetone
bath, which dissolved the remaining PMMA and detached the gold film deposited on top of it. Only
the gold deposited directly inside the open PMMA windows remained adhered to the silicon,
yielding the free-standing split-ring resonator array.
